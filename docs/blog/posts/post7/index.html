Are poor countries catching up?

For most of the last sixty years, no. Since 2000, yes, and whether you count countries or people changes the answer by twenty years.

Textbook growth theory makes a simple prediction: poor countries should grow faster than rich ones, because capital is scarce where they are and technology can be borrowed rather than invented. If that holds, the gap between rich and poor countries shrinks over time, which economists call convergence.

The question: in which decades did poor countries actually grow faster than rich ones, and does the answer change if we count people instead of countries?

The data

Penn World Table 11.0 covers 185 economies from 1950 to 2023. For each decade since 1960, I take every country with at least a million people and compute two things. Income at the start of the decade uses PWT’s expenditure-side GDP at purchasing-power parity, divided by population, which is built to compare living standards across countries. Growth over the decade uses national-accounts GDP per person, which is built to compare a country with itself over time, expressed as an average annual rate. The 2010s stop in 2019, before the pandemic. Using two different series also keeps any error in the income level from mechanically showing up in the growth rate.

Sixty years in one chart

Each bubble is a country, sized by population. Drag the slider or press play to move through the decades; hover over a bubble to see which country it is; click a continent in the legend to hide or isolate it. The dashed line is the fitted relationship for that decade, with every country counting once.

In the 1960s the line slopes up: the richest quarter of countries grew 3.3 percent a year and the poorest quarter 2.0. The world was diverging. The 1980s and 1990s are flat. Then in the 2000s the line tips clearly downward: the poorest quarter grew 3.5 percent a year against 1.4 for the richest. The 2010s keep the downward slope, though less steeply.

Countries or people

Every country counts once in that fit, so Malawi weighs as much as India. Counting people instead, by weighting each country by its population, tells a different story:

Weighted by people, convergence starts in the 1980s, twenty years earlier, with a slope of -1.08 against -0.02 for countries. Go back to the first chart, set it to the 1980s and hover over the two largest bubbles: China and India, among the poorest countries in 1980, growing at 7 and 3 percent a year. Then click everything but Africa off in the legend: 20 of Africa’s 38 economies shrank per person through that decade. Together those two facts are the whole gap between the lines.

What it means

Whether the world is converging depends on what you are asking. If the question is about people, about how many of the world’s poor live in economies that are catching up, the answer has been yes since the 1980s, largely because of two countries. If the question is whether a typical poor country can expect to catch up, the answer was no for forty years and has been yes only since about 2000, a result Patel, Sandefur and Subramanian (2021) find across several datasets. It is still young, and it was weaker in the 2010s than in the 2000s.

These are unconditional comparisons: they do not hold education, institutions or commodity prices fixed, and the set of countries with data grows from 91 in the 1960s to 150 in the 2010s.
